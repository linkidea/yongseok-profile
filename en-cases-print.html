CASE 01 · Risk · Audit

Risk as the company's language,
not one person's experience

Three months that turned after-the-fact response into an enterprise risk map and always-on monitoring

01BACKGROUND — THE PROBLEM

Risk management rested on each department's own experience, applied after the fact.

  • Siloed — each department managed its own risks; no shared standard or language
  • Reactive — checked and acted only after a problem surfaced; recovery over prevention
  • Person-dependent — the quality of management varied with whoever was in charge
  • Trigger — repeated incidents and audit findings exposed the need for a company-wide standard and monitoring

02APPROACH — HOW

  • Risk map — defined 118 risk elements across 11 core work areas and organised them into a risk map for each business.
  • Lifecycle — built a template for identify → assess → respond → monitor → close, and ran a quarterly review.
  • Audit linkage — fed the risk map into audit planning and prioritisation, which shaped audit scope and led to a revision of the purchasing management policy.
  • Anomaly detection — monitored threshold-based signals including sales mix, counterparty credit rating, changes in transaction volume, new counterparties, concentration and transactions with closed businesses. I used AI to help build the logic; when a figure looked wrong, the relevant division was asked to explain and to act.

03RESULT — IN NUMBERS

11 core work areas · risk management units
118 risk elements · risk map by business
7 divisions and subsidiaries · quarterly review

04WHAT THIS CASE SHOWS

  • The point of preventive audit — not catching people, but defining risk in advance and turning it into a standing signal.
  • The advantage of an auditor from the business — turning diagnosis results into a structure, then agreeing it with the divisions and operating it.
  • Execution — resolving data-access constraints and standards negotiations inside three months.

CASE 02 · Audit · Ethics

How one audit becomes
something the company keeps

Analysing misconduct and ethics cases, structuring causes and warning signs, and sharing them with divisions as written guidance and video

01BACKGROUND — THE PROBLEM

When an audit ends with catching individual cases, the same kind of misconduct repeats in another division.

  • Detection-centred — confirming a case and closing it leaves the cause and the warning signs with nobody
  • Cases not shared — the same type of risk surfaces again in a different division
  • No field language — audit reports are hard for the field to read, so they rarely turn into prevention

02APPROACH — HOW

  • Scope — embezzlement, kickbacks, conflicts of interest, false claims, misuse of corporate cards
  • Framework — the Fraud Diamond (opportunity, pressure, rationalisation, capability) to structure causes
  • Content — written guidance and a video, in language the field can follow
  • Link to audit — warning signs found in diagnosis were verified through case analysis and fed back as guidance

03RESULT — IN NUMBERS

5 categories of misconduct · embezzlement, kickbacks, conflicts, false claims, cards
2 formats of guidance · written and video
7 divisions and subsidiaries reached

04WHAT THIS CASE SHOWS

  • Audit is for prevention, not detection — leaving behind the cause and the warning signs, not just the case.
  • Sharing cases is learning, not warning — so the same type does not repeat in another division.
  • The advantage of an auditor from the business — understanding the operational context of a case and explaining it in the field's own language.

CASE 03 · Purchasing · PI · Data

Purchasing that leaves data behind,
not just experience

KRW 13B next-generation system, purchasing workstream PM — quotations, bidding, contracts and evaluation in one data flow

01BACKGROUND — THE PROBLEM

Bidding data accumulated but was never used for analysis.

  • Scattered data — purchasing data lived on personal PCs and in spreadsheets, never becoming an organisational asset
  • Split process — quotations, bidding, contracts and evaluation were managed separately, so the whole flow was hard to see
  • Manual control — vendor credit and licence checks, and collusion screening, depended on manual work after the fact
  • Weak audit trail — no record of why a vendor was selected or at what unit price the contract was signed

02APPROACH — HOW

  • Bidding system — designed so that bidding data could be analysed as a database rather than archived as documents.
  • Cost DB — made bid history and the cost database available together when estimating pre-bid prices.
  • Financial linkage — connected sales entries with purchase entries so actual margin could be checked per transaction.
  • Vendor lookup — displayed credit information automatically in the vendor lookup screen.
  • Collusion detection — implemented IP-overlap checks and bid-rate analysis, and the screening surfaced signs of collusion in live reviews.
  • Leadership — led a 15-person project team and ran change management and user training alongside delivery.

03RESULT — IN NUMBERS

KRW 13B next-generation system · purchasing workstream PM
25% reduction in purchasing processing time
85% user satisfaction · 15-person team led

04WHAT THIS CASE SHOWS

  • Purchasing as a structure of data and control — not individual negotiating skill, but a structure where data stays and can be analysed.
  • Design experience that carries into audit — understanding where control is weak at the design stage.
  • What an auditor from the business adds — someone who has built controls knows where they leak.

CASE 04 · Strategy

From strategy to service,
from service to revenue

2021–2022 strategy planning — benchmarking leading overseas players → embedding the findings in strategy → launching proptech and mobile services, KRW 5B in cumulative revenue

01BACKGROUND — THE PROBLEM

When strategy stays on paper, the organisation does not move.

  • Limited view — a domestic-only perspective made new growth paths hard to find
  • Gap to execution — strategy, services and KPIs moving separately rarely turn into results
  • Need to benchmark — comparing and analysing the service models of leading overseas players was necessary to inform strategy

02APPROACH — HOW

  • Overseas benchmarking — compared service models, revenue structures and customer touchpoints
  • Into strategy — turned the findings into the mid-term strategy and concrete new-service initiatives
  • Service launch — proptech and mobile services
  • Into execution — KPI management, executive decision support, Bain & Company PMO

03RESULT — IN NUMBERS

KRW 5B cumulative revenue from new services
Proptech service launched
Mobile service launched

04WHAT THIS CASE SHOWS

  • Strategy is proven by services and revenue, not reports — benchmarking was carried through to launched services.
  • Turning benchmarking into strategy — not stopping at comparison and analysis, but shaping it into company initiatives.
  • Connecting executive language to field execution — strategy, KPIs and services pointed in the same direction.

CASE 05 · Automation · Data

From 100% manual to automated,
from 5 minutes to 3

Moving counterparty financial, shareholder and customer data collection to an in-house system — and solving an external data constraint directly when it appeared mid-operation

01BACKGROUND — THE PROBLEM

The counterparty credit information that diagnosis and audit depend on was collected entirely by hand.

  • What was collected — counterparty financials, shareholder and ownership structure, and customer concentration, looked up case by case
  • How — search the data site, read the screen, type it up. Five minutes per case
  • Structural problem — repetitive work and constant room for error, eating into the analyst's actual analysis time
  • Starting point — selected from five productivity candidates, with a target of at least 15% less time per case

02APPROACH — HOW

  • Verification scope — with repetitive collection and comparison handled by the system, the analyst focuses on interpreting anomalies rather than gathering data.
  • Extension — the same approach grew into automated anomaly detection for counterparties.
  • Durability — when an external data constraint appeared, I rebuilt a workaround myself instead of waiting it out.

03RESULT — IN NUMBERS

5 → 3 min time per case · information collection
40% reduction · against a 15% target
100% manual process converted · data stored in a database

04WHAT THIS CASE SHOWS

  • Wider verification, not just less work — repetitive collection and comparison goes to the system, and people interpret the anomalies. The point of automation is wider verification.
  • Extending the approach — the same method developed into automated counterparty anomaly detection.
  • Making results last — when external data constraints appeared in operation, I rebuilt a workaround rather than waiting.
